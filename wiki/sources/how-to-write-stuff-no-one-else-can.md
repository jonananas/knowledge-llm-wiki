# How To Write Stuff No One Else Can

Source: [The Write to Roam](https://thewritetoroam.com/2024/02/how-to-write-stuff-no-one-else-can). Raw capture: [raw/how-to-write-stuff-no-one-else-can.md](../../raw/how-to-write-stuff-no-one-else-can.md).

## Summary

Ethan Brooks argues that distinctive writing comes from [[information-advantage]]: knowing things competitors do not know. His preferred route is access to overlooked people who were close to an event, company, or creative project but are not the public face of it.

The article is partly a theory of writing moats and partly a practical research method. Brooks suggests mapping company timelines, identifying employees around major turning points, searching for their public traces, and using interviews or consulting calls to uncover context that does not appear in conventional coverage.

## Key Takeaways

- There are three broad competitive advantages for creative work: resources, behavior, and information.
- Brooks argues that information advantage is the one writers can most deliberately cultivate.
- The strongest information advantage often comes from people, not documents: especially behind-the-scenes contributors with direct experience and little attention.
- Competitors tend to either rehash public material or chase the famous person at the center of a story; better access may come from agents, editors, operators, early employees, interns, and other close collaborators.
- Original reporting can come from neglected locations as well as people: archives, old newspapers, niche communities, and obscure records.
- Manual source mapping matters because it builds contextual judgment about the relationship between people, timing, and company milestones.
- A small number of well-chosen conversations can save many hours of surface-level research.

## Method

For company research, Brooks starts by plotting major growth milestones from founder interviews and public material. He then searches LinkedIn for people who worked at the company near those turning points, recording names, titles, departments, and dates. The resulting map suggests what the company was prioritizing and which overlooked people may have seen the important work up close.

After building the map, he searches names in Google and Spotify to find prior interviews or other public traces. He also treats paid calls with former employees as a practical way to access context. The final move is [[interview-chaining]]: ask each source who else should be consulted.
