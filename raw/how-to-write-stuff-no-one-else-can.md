# How To Write Stuff No One Else Can

Source URL: https://thewritetoroam.com/2024/02/how-to-write-stuff-no-one-else-can
Author: Ethan Brooks
Published: 2024-02-09
Captured: 2026-08-26

Ethan Brooks argues that writers can create work competitors cannot easily copy by cultivating an information advantage. The most durable form of that advantage is access to under-attended people: employees, collaborators, operators, and specialists who were close to important events but do not receive public attention.

## Main Points

- Creative businesses can compete through resources, behavior, or information.
- Information advantage is the most deliberately cultivable advantage and may be the most resilient in an AI-heavy writing environment.
- The most valuable information often comes from people behind the visible figure: agents, editors, operators, early employees, interns, and other contributors close to the work.
- Public attention concentrates around founders, CEOs, celebrities, authors, and lead performers, even when less visible collaborators have equal or better operational insight.
- Distinctive stories often require going where other writers do not: niche groups, old newspapers, archives, obscure public records, and overlooked source networks.
- For company research, Brooks starts with founder interviews and major milestones, then maps employees around those turning points using LinkedIn.
- He manually records names, roles, departments, and dates because the process builds contextual judgment that automated collection can miss.
- Once a map exists, he searches for lesser-known key employees in Google and Spotify, looking for interviews, podcasts, talks, and other public traces.
- Former employees can often be reached directly for paid consulting-style conversations, yielding context that would otherwise be expensive or impossible to infer.
- A useful final interview question is: "Who else do you think I should talk to about this?"

## Examples

The article uses Tim Ferriss's acknowledgements in *The 4-Hour Workweek* to show how crucial contributors can hide in plain sight. Brooks notes that people thanked as agents, editors, marketers, publicity staff, or interns may have unusually strong insight into why a project worked.

It also uses The Hustle's Sunday Story format, refined by Zachary Crockett, as an example of writing that succeeded because it drew from overlooked sources rather than the same common links everyone else used.

## Practical Workflow

1. Identify the company, project, or event you want to understand.
2. Read founder or leader interviews to sketch the growth timeline and major turning points.
3. Use LinkedIn to map employees around those turning points.
4. Capture name, role, department, and start/end dates in a spreadsheet.
5. Look for public traces from overlooked people who were close to the action.
6. Interview or consult with a small number of these people.
7. Ask every good source who else you should speak with.
